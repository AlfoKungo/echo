Opening Echo…

A little practice, your way.

Practise mixes a little of everything. Words is your vocabulary, as cards, Endless and the Walkman tape; under a card you choose how you practise it: Read, Listen or Speak. Grammar teaches a topic and then practises it; Verbs drills conjugation on its own. Listen plays French and asks what you understood, trains your ear on twin sounds, and has a sentence tape and RFI’s daily news in slow French; Read shows French and asks the same. Story is a serial written for you, and Radio is a short episode each day. Hands-free gathers the three tapes and today’s Radio for when you’re out and about. Each room’s ⓘ explains it in full. The Study links at the bottom of Home include Mistakes, which gathers what keeps catching you out, and Statistics, which opens with the points of the last two weeks, words and verbs together (tap a day to see how it split), then shows one part at a time — Words, Verbs or Other, and it remembers which. Words: how their scores spread from shaky to solid (tap a bar to see its words; choose a day, or press ▶, to see the chart as it was at the end of any day since you started), how many you know, your hardest words, each category, and your answers over the last two weeks. Verbs: the same score chart for your verb forms, your hardest forms (tap one to fill in its table), each tense and person with its average score and share right, and each kind of verb. Other: your Listen and Read levels and the rest. Every chart’s ⓘ says what it shows. Nothing there is a target.

Cards. Recall the word, then tap to reveal its translation. Tap the left half if you didn’t know it, or the right half if you did. Space or Enter reveals; the arrow keys grade. Undo takes back your latest answer.

Two successful recalls after your latest miss finish a practice cycle — or just one, when a word comes back and you know it straight away, if you finished its last cycle without a miss or its score (as in Statistics) is 4 or more. A miss resets the count to zero and moves the card to the failed stack. FSRS schedules the next review. Each direction has its own memory. Category, daily allowance and pronunciation speed live in Words options (•••).

Your working deck has three stacks: unseen, last answered incorrectly, and last answered correctly. Unseen counts the first stack plus new words available within your daily allowance. Seen counts the other two stacks together. A due word you know straight away finishes there if you didn’t miss it last time or its score is high; any other leaves Seen after two right answers in a row, so Seen often reads 0. Finished cards count in neither. Undo restores the card, its stack and the counts.

The first ten unseen cards come first. After that, how often an unseen card comes depends on how many seen cards are waiting: up to three in four while the pile is small, one in two at 25 seen, one in three at 33, one in five at 41, and never fewer than one in ten — so the pile fills to about 25–35 and stays there, a little higher on a day of many misses, and new cards never stop. Among seen cards, failed ones come twice as often as successful ones; empty stacks are skipped. A card you just missed waits for at least three others; one you got right waits until every other seen card has had a turn (30 cards when 30 are seen; at least 5, at most 40), so seeing it again really tests you. If every seen card is still waiting, the one that has waited longest comes back rather than an extra new word, so new words keep to their share; the waits shorten further only near the end, when nothing else is left. Every card enters or re-enters a stack at a random position. The daily allowance renews at 04:00 on your Mac; unfinished cards keep their stacks and progress. It is shared across categories and moves in steps of 5; at 0 no new words come, and due reviews are never capped.

Endless (a tab in Words) goes over the words you have already started, as long as you like, with no unseen or seen stacks and never a new word. It picks them the way the Walkman does: the lower a word’s score, the likelier it comes — so the ones you find hard come most — and a word waits at least ten others before it comes back. Knowing a word adds 1 to its score and missing it takes 1 off, the same score the Walkman and Statistics use; your daily stacks, counts and review schedule stay exactly as they were. Undo takes back the last answer. It follows the direction and category you have chosen.

Board (a tab in Words) lays out the words of the category you have chosen as tiles in a new order each visit, for the direction you have chosen; the shuffle button lays them out again. Board options (Words options, •••, then Board) chooses which. Only words in Cards (on at first) keeps to the ones in your Cards right now, up to 100, missed ones first; off, every word of the category — on All words, your whole deck. The score range keeps to the words whose score (as in Statistics, rounded) is in it — −8 or lower to 0 shows the ones you find hard. A word starts at 0 and stays there until you first answer it, so the words you have just added show whenever 0 is in the range. The full range, −8 or lower to 8 or higher, shows every word; any narrower range leaves out words you have never started. Both are remembered on this device. Tap a tile to see its meaning for 2 seconds; tap it again to turn it back sooner. A tap also plays the word when Say the French when a card turns is on (Words options). French is coloured by gender, and words scoring below 0 are outlined in red. It changes no score or schedule.

Extra practice. Add 15, 20, 30 or any number of new words for today only; your daily allowance is unchanged and the extra ends at 04:00. You can also review finished words early — 15, 20, 30 or any number of the ones due soonest, or the ones you missed most. At the end of your cards, choose what and how many, and the button says exactly what it will do. In Your words, More for today offers the same and asks you to confirm. Asking for more than are available takes all of them. At the end of your cards, Hardest right now lists up to five finished words whose score is below 0, lowest first (the score Endless and the Walkman go by); Review these brings them back at once. An early review is a real review: finishing it updates the word’s schedule, with a smaller step because it came early.

Your words (the search button in Words) lists every word, with Word families, Word building and More for today at the top. Choose Studied to see the words you have practised, most recent first, with when you last studied each, how often you missed it and its next review. Hide translations lets you test yourself and reveal each answer; checking the list never changes your progress.

Word families (in Your words) groups words built from one another — la cuisine, cuisiner, le cuisinier — with a note on how each is built. It lists the families where you already know a word, fullest first; tap one to see its words, hear them, and add one you haven’t started. Because a word whose relative you know is half known, up to half of each day’s new words come from families you have started; the rest follow the usual order, so every category keeps moving. Word building (in Your words, at the top of Grammar, or from Word families) shows the dozen patterns these families are made by — -er makes the verb, -eur the person who does it, in- the opposite, two words joined into one — each with pairs of your own words; tap a word to hear it, read how it is built, or add it.

Read · Listen · Speak, under the card in Cards and Endless (French → English), is how you practise the card. Read shows the French. Listen says it and keeps it hidden until the card turns. Once a card turns, its word stays where it was and the answer appears under it (a word with a long note moves up only as far as the note needs); the speaker at its top right says the French again. Speak shows and says it, and you say the English out loud (below). In English → French you always read. Say the French when a card turns (Words options, •••; off at first) plays the recording when you turn a card yourself as you Read, in English → French, or when you tap a tile on Board. The Listen and Read rooms always play their recordings; the pronunciation speed in Words options — normal or slower — applies everywhere.

Walkman (a tab in Words, and on Home under Hands-free) plays a French word, pauses so you can find its meaning, says the English, then the French again — word after word, and it keeps playing with your phone locked. Every eighth word is new, heard three times: again about 10 words later and once more about 20 after that, so it never comes straight back. The rest are words you have started, picked one of two ways, chosen at the top. By score (the default): every word has a score that rises when you recall it (+2) or hear it to the end on the Walkman (+0.5), drops when you miss it (−3), and fades by half every three weeks; words with low scores are far likelier to be picked — a word at −6 about 130 times likelier than one at +6 — but any word can come up. By groups: words you are learning first (the ones you miss most at the front), then those due for review, then known words about to fade. Either way a word waits at least 15 others before it returns, and listening never changes your vocabulary schedule. Heard, under the player, lists the last 50 finished words and sentences, newest first: the speaker plays one again (the tape pauses and carries on), ✓ and ✗ let you say afterwards whether you knew it — counted like a recall (+2) or a miss (−3) in its score, tap again to take it back — and a word you haven’t started can go straight into your practice. Marks move only the Walkman score, never your vocabulary schedule.

Speak lets you answer out loud: Echo shows and says the French, you say its English, and Echo checks it, turns the card and grades it for you. Tap Start speaking (or the card) once — iPhone opens the microphone only on a tap. You have 40 seconds to start your answer — thinking aloud doesn't use them up faster — and Echo listens until you have answered and stopped; your Mac writes down what you said and checks it against the word's meanings, generously: any one of them counts, "the" or "to" or not, a plural for a singular, spaces and hyphens don't matter ("far away" for "faraway"), a long answer may have one word put another way ("piece of clothing" for "item of clothing"), and anything that sounds like it counts — "live" for "leave", "hi" for "eye", even a word recognition made up — since recognition mixes those up far more often than you do. When the words still don't match, your Mac checks the right answer against your recording itself: if it fits nearly as well as what was written down, you said it and recognition misread it, so it counts as right. Right is a rising chime and counts as Knew it; wrong, "I don't know" (said, or the Don't know button) or no answer in 40 seconds is a falling chime and the English, and counts as Didn’t know — exactly as a tap would: in Cards a grade that schedules the word, in Endless +1 or −1. The turned card shows the verdict for a moment, with the half it chose marked; tap a half then to grade it yourself instead. Tapping the card before you answer turns it by hand, as usual. Thinking aloud ("let me think", "just a…") and anything it couldn't make out count for nothing, and it listens on. If Echo heard you wrong, tap I said it right (it stays until your next answer): that answer is marked again as known, and what Echo heard counts for that word from then on. The recordings of answers that needed that second check — the latest 200 — stay on your Mac, so the check can be tuned to your voice. Keep the screen on and Echo open: iPhone turns the microphone off when the screen locks, and Speak pauses until you tap the card. While the microphone is open iPhone plays sound at phone-call quality; earphones may sound better.

Songs (a tab in Words) turns a song into words: search for a French song, pick the right version (covers that sing the same words are grouped together), and Claude (Opus) reads it in the background, usually in under a minute — each word in its dictionary form: the infinitive for a verb, the masculine singular for an adjective, a noun with le, la or l’ and its gender. Names and sounds are left out. A song opens on its lyrics, line by line: › at a line’s start gives its meaning, and a tapped word its note. The row under the title opens the song’s words: you untick anything you don’t want, and the rest become a deck named after the song — including the words you already have, which join it as they are: they keep their own category and your progress on them, so practising the song covers all of it. Each word joins your practice once it has been recorded, before your other new words or mixed in with them, as you choose on the Songs tab. The words page also says how much of what is sung you already know. The lyrics stay on your Mac.

Story is a serial in French, one episode at a time, written for you by Claude (Opus) from what you know: nearly every word is one you practise or have understood in Listen and Read, with a handful of new words and one grammar point a step ahead. Tap a word for its meaning (a new one says so), or › at the end of a paragraph for its translation. Listen reads the episode aloud — in French only, or each line in French and then in English, as you choose beneath it; the line playing is marked at its left edge, it keeps playing with your phone locked, and pausing or tapping a word stops it at that line. The three questions follow the reading. When you finish, the next episode is written: a step harder if you looked up little and answered everything right, a step easier if you looked up a lot or missed questions, and the words you looked up come back in it. Writing takes a few minutes.

Radio is one episode a day, a few minutes long, made on your Mac: today’s words in sentences, each followed by its meaning; the latest story episode, line by line with its English; and one grammar point, explained in English with French examples. Like the Walkman it keeps playing with your phone locked, and the lock-screen buttons skip a part at a time. A new episode is ready each morning after 04:00.

Verbs drills conjugation on its own, with the verbs in your vocabulary (song verbs too). The Practising line at the top says which verbs and tenses the drills use; tap it, or •••, to choose your verbs (the ones you have started, the 20 most common, or your own pick) and the tenses — présent, passé composé, imparfait, futur. Cards works like your vocabulary cards: a verb, a tense and a person; say the form to yourself, tap to turn the card (it shows the form and the whole tense), then tap left if you didn’t know it and right if you did (or Space, then the arrow keys). Your mark counts for that form just like a typed answer: it colours your verbs’ squares and decides which forms come up next, and a form you missed comes back a few cards later. Cards never run out: do as many as you like and leave whenever you want. Type it asks one form at a time and, like Cards, never ends — more forms are chosen as you go, from how your answers are going: a wrong answer says which mistake it was (another person’s form, another tense, a doubled letter, the wrong helper), shows the whole tense with the endings lit, and comes back once, a few forms later. Accents are not required, here or in Whole table: the right letters with an accent missing or wrong count as right, and the form is shown with its accents. Whole table asks all six persons of one verb at once. Next table (the default) picks the table for you from the verbs and tenses you chose, tables with shaky or untried forms most often and never one of the last five, and after Check moves on to the next; Choose lets you pick the verb and tense yourself, as does Fill in this table on a verb’s page. By ear plays a short phrase and asks which of the tenses you practise it is — so it asks that only when you practise two or more — or, in the présent and the imparfait, whether it is about one person or more; many endings are silent, so sometimes the honest answer is “can’t tell by ear”. Every form has a score, like a word: +2 for a typed answer right and −3 wrong (Type it, Whole table), +1 or −2 for your own mark (Cards, In sentences, the verb tape), fading back towards 0 over a few weeks; By ear does not count. Cards, Type it, Next table and the verb tape all pick by it, the lowest scores most often, and Statistics shows the scores day by day, the points of each day, the average by tense and by person, and your five hardest forms. Catching you out lists the forms you have missed most; tap one to practise its table. Each row under Tenses shows your forms in that tense, from solid to shaky, and opens a page for it: what it is for, the pattern on three model verbs with the changing part lit, your own verbs sorted by what is regular about them there (irregular verbs, stem changes, verbs like finir, partir or prendre, être verbs, irregular participles and futur stems), and the full rules from Grammar. All your verbs finds any verb by its French or English and shows every form, one square per person, from shaky to solid: it starts with the verbs you have practised, shakiest first, and the tenses you practise; Shaky, Not practised and All change the list, and Show all 4 tenses adds the rest. Tap a verb to see it in all four tenses, or a square to practise its table. Claude (Opus) writes each verb’s table once, and Echo checks it against the rules French is regular about before using it; the By ear phrases are recorded and checked on your Mac in the background.

Form → verb, the switch above the Cards, turns them the other way: the card shows a form on its own (mens), and you name its verb, tense and person. The turned card shows the verb and tense, every person with that form (je mens, tu mens), and any other verb or tense it could be, with those persons lit in the table. Your mark counts for the form the card was drawn for: +1 if you knew it, −1 if not, since knowing a form when you see it is the easier half. A phrase built on a verb (avoir peur) shows as the verb itself.

In sentences puts the verb inside a short French sentence: the card shows the sentence with one blank and the verb’s infinitive, and a word or the context tells you the tense — hier soir for the passé composé, quand j’étais petit for the imparfait, demain for the futur, d’habitude for the présent. Say the whole sentence to yourself, then tap the card: it fills the blank, plays the sentence, gives its English, and says under the card which cue sets the tense. Then left or right, as in Cards; your mark counts for that form like a Cards mark (+1 if you knew it, −2 if not), and a sentence you missed comes back a few cards later. It uses the verbs and tenses on the Practising line; with several tenses chosen, the passé composé comes most, then présent, imparfait and futur. The sentences are written ahead by Claude (Opus), twelve at a time, with the words you have started, leaning on your shaky forms; each one is kept only if its blank holds exactly the form in Echo’s table for that verb, tense and person. Change the line and Claude writes sentences for it; those for other verbs or tenses wait until you choose them again. Your Mac records each sentence before it shows, and apart from a miss coming back, you see every sentence once before any is shown again.

Look, then write is for forms you have never seen written. It picks a table the way Next table does and shows it filled in first, the endings in colour and the rule under it. Tap Next · write it and the same table comes back empty, to fill in from memory; Check marks it, and Next table moves to another verb, shown first again. The table is graded as a whole, in your points: +2 if every form is right, −1 if any is wrong or blank. It doesn’t change the forms’ own scores. Check again, here or in Whole table, never counts.

How it’s built drills how each tense is made, not single forms. A card asks for a verb’s présent stems (one for parler: parl-; two or three for others: vien- ven- vienn-; être, avoir, aller and faire by their forms), its imparfait stem (with its nous form as the clue: nous finissons → finiss-, and être’s ét-), its futur stem (prendr-, and the irregular ones: ser-, aur-, ir-, viendr-…), or whether it takes être or avoir in the passé composé and what its participle is (passer, monter, descendre, sortir and retourner take both: être on their own, avoir with an object, and either counts as right everywhere in Verbs). Turn it to see the answer, three example forms, the whole tense with the endings lit and the rule; where the verb tape or By ear has recorded the examples, they play as the card turns. Tap left if you didn’t know it and right if you did (or Space, then the arrow keys); a missed card comes back once, a few cards later. The cards follow the Practising line, like Cards: only the verbs and tenses chosen there, the irregular ones first. Each card keeps its own score, +1 or −2 per mark, fading over a few weeks, and the cards you missed or haven’t seen come up most often. A mark also counts for every form of that tense, +4 or −4 each, so it moves those forms in Statistics and your points.

The verb tape (Tape, in Verbs) drills conjugation by ear: you hear a verb, a tense and a person — the person in English (“the they-form”), because il and ils sound the same — then a pause to say the form in your head, then the form twice. It uses the 20 most common verbs, in the tenses you choose under the player; they are the same tenses as in Verbs, so changing one changes both. Forms you got wrong come early, solid ones late. In Heard, ✓ or ✗ on a form counts like a Verbs card, and once marked it stays marked.

Change it, the other side of the switch at the top of Tape in Verbs, drills whole sentences by ear. You hear a short French sentence, then a change — a time (Hier, Demain, Quand j’étais petit, D’habitude) that puts it in another tense, or another subject (nous, ils, vous) — then a pause as long as the answer takes to say, to say the new sentence, then the answer. It uses the verbs and tenses on the Practising line in Verbs, the passé composé most when you chose several. A time moves the sentence from one of your tenses to another, so with one tense chosen Change it changes only the person. Claude (Opus) writes the sentences ahead for those verbs, with the words you have started, leaning on forms you got wrong; Echo checks every verb form against its own tables and leaves out any sentence that doesn’t match, and a sentence plays once its three recordings are made. Sentences you haven’t heard come first. In Heard, ✓ or ✗ on a sentence counts for the verb form that changed, like a Verbs card.

When your Mac can’t be reached — a dropped connection, or the Mac too busy to answer — Echo says so and keeps trying by itself (after 2, 4, 8 and 15 seconds, then every 30), and at once when your phone is back online or you return to the app. What you were doing waits for it: an answer is kept and saved as soon as the Mac answers, and never counted twice. Retry now tries straight away. When the Mac is only slow, cards, Listen, Read and Practise go straight on — your result and the next question show at once while your answers save behind, with a quiet “Saving…” at the top.

Colours and hooks. Nouns are coloured by gender — blue for masculine, pink for feminine — wherever Echo shows French (switch it off in Words options, •••). A word you keep missing gets a short memory hook on its card, written by Claude, to help it stick; so does a verb form that stays at −2 or lower after three misses, shown on its turned card in Cards, after a wrong answer in Type it, and under a checked table.

Listen and Read never end. Each question is chosen for you from graded French — single sentences, short dialogues and short texts, A1 towards B1. You choose what a sentence means, or answer a question about a passage. The small A1 · A2 · B1 label shows the level Echo is choosing from: it drifts up while you keep answering well and eases back when you don’t. Nothing is scored or counted.

Tap a word. Every French word Echo has a meaning for is underlined: tap it to see what it means in that sentence, and Add to my words puts it into your vocabulary practice (it tells you if the word is already there, or makes it due again). In Read, looking a word up before you answer counts as help, so a right answer is recorded as “with help”. After you answer, tap as freely as you like.

After each answer you see whether you were right, the expected answer, a short explanation, and the whole French with its English, line by line. One button continues; Enter does the same.

In Listen the recording plays by itself when the question appears, and a passage plays line by line with the line number while it runs. The speaker button pauses and continues; Line again replays just the line you last heard and stops after it, and From the start plays it all again. The French stays hidden until you answer. If your browser blocks playback, a large play button appears — tap it once and Echo carries on by itself.

Grammar by ear comes up in Questions after every two questions, when one is ready: a short French sentence and a question that only its grammar answers — when it happens (the tense of the verb), how many people do it (ils prennent sounds different from il prend), or who it is done to or whose it is (lui or leur, le or la, son or leur). Claude (Opus) writes them ahead, eight at a time, from the verbs and words you have started, most often in the passé composé, then the présent, the imparfait and the futur. Echo checks every verb form against its own tables and keeps only questions you can answer by ear — never il mange against ils mangent, which sound the same — and a question waits until its sentence is recorded on your Mac. After you answer you see why, then the French with its English. A question you miss comes back once, after three more Grammar by ear questions. These answers change no Listen level and no score.

Sounds trains your ear on pairs of phrases that differ by one sound — le chat and les chats, tu and tout, bon and banc. Echo plays one; you choose which you heard, then can hear both. The pairs you confuse come back first. Every recording was checked to be heard as itself and not its twin; a pair whose recording failed that check is left out.

Tape (in Listen, and on Home under Hands-free) plays the Listen and Read sentences up to your Read level the way the Walkman plays words — French, a pause to understand it, the English, the French again — each once per tape, with sentences holding your due and shakiest words likelier to come first. It keeps playing with your phone locked, hearing the sentences changes no scores, and Heard lists them too.

News is RFI’s Journal en français facile: ten minutes of the day’s world news in slow French, read by RFI’s presenters every weekday, with RFI’s own transcript. Your Mac fetches each new edition within half an hour, from the day News started on it (earlier editions are not fetched), and keeps the latest 14. It times every line against the recording (Whisper, on the Mac, in a minute or two), then Claude (Opus) translates every line and writes a note for its words, which takes a while; the transcript and the recording are there meanwhile. Claude also marks where each story starts, and gives it a heading. An edition reads like a song’s lyrics: › at a line’s start shows what it means, and a tapped word takes its colour and shows its note under the line (tap elsewhere to close it). › and ▶ show only on the line playing and the line you last touched. The bar at the bottom names the stories (tap one to go to it; the one playing is lit), plays and pauses, goes back a line, on to the next, or plays this line again, and switches between normal speed and 0.8×; English in the bar shows every line’s English at once, and stays as you left it. ▶ beside a line plays from that line, the line being read is marked, and it keeps playing with your phone locked; the lock-screen buttons skip a line at a time. When you scroll away from the line playing, Back to line above the bar brings you back to it. The words you tap are listed at the end of the edition, on this device; Add all to Cards has Claude put them in their dictionary form (la raffinerie, not raffineries) and adds them to a News deck in Words; a word you already have joins that deck as it is, keeping its progress. At most 40 words go at a time. The line you reach is kept on your Mac, so an edition opens where you left it, and the list shows each edition with its first stories, a dot while it is new, a line for how far you played it, or Finished. A step that fails says why under the edition and is tried again half an hour later. Nothing is scored.

Sentences: hear a French sentence, read it, or both (your choice stays on this phone). Think what it means, then tap the card for the English — and the French, if you only heard it. Tap a word for its meaning, the speaker to hear it again. Not yet brings it back a few cards later and again tomorrow; Got it spaces it out over days, then weeks. The sentences keep to the tenses on your Practising line in Verbs and the grammar topics you leave on (•••), with at most one word you haven’t met. Nothing is scored.

Practise is one endless stream that alternates understanding, vocabulary and grammar: a comprehension question, a vocabulary card, another comprehension question, a grammar question, skipping anything with nothing waiting; every other grammar turn is a Write it sentence instead, an English sentence to write in French. The small label says which you are in. Grammar here is a quiet round across the topics you have already touched, and it never shows a score.

Mistakes gathers what keeps catching you out: a grammar topic you miss, words scoring below 0 now (weakest first; a word leaves once you have fixed it), sentences you didn’t catch, and verb forms at a score of −2 or lower. Tap a group to practise just that — a grammar group alternates its grammar questions with the sentences that use it, the words group the sentences that use those words, and Verb forms asks those forms in Type it until none is left at −2.

Grammar. Seven units take you from A1 towards B1. Open a topic to read its rule — a summary, with each part folded under its title — then practise a round of 10 questions: build, rewrite, complete, repair, choose, translate and conjugate. Check your answer, try again with specific feedback, or ask for a hint or the rule; each round records what you did on your own, with help, or need to revisit. Ctrl/⌘ + Enter checks or continues. Once a question is answered, a speaker button reads the correct French aloud where a recording exists.

Continue, at the top, lists every round and test you have left open, with the question you reached. To revisit under them counts the questions you missed or needed help with, across your topics; it starts a round that leads with those (the same round Practise draws its grammar from). Next up is the first topic you haven’t started in the unit you are working through. Each unit folds: only the one you are working in is open, and a tap opens any other. A topic’s bar shows its questions answered on your own, those to revisit, and the rest not yet tried. By level lists the topics as A1, A2 and B1 instead.

Tests. A topic test asks 15 questions across the whole topic, and each unit ends with a test of 20 questions across its topics. Tests have no hints, rule or shown answers, and each question takes one try; you see the right answer and why straight after. Your best and latest scores are kept, and anything you miss in a test comes back first the next time you practise that topic.

Write it (under Also, at the bottom of Grammar, and every other grammar turn in Practise) shows a short English sentence to write in French. The sentences are written ahead by Claude (Opus), eight at a time, from the words you have started and the grammar topics you are weakest in, so each one practises a topic named under it. Check sends your sentence to Claude, which takes a few seconds: any correct way of saying it counts, and you see your sentence corrected with the fewest changes, what was wrong and why, and how a French speaker would say it. Write it again lets you try once more; Skip this one moves on. Nothing here is scored.

Your vocabulary progress, grammar rounds, comprehension levels and mistakes live on your Mac, shared by your phone and desktop. Your direction, category, audio choices and theme (dark Dusk, light Daylight or neon Vice, above) are remembered on this device. Echo always opens on Home, and Back returns to the previous screen.
