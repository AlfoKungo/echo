Opening Echo…

A little practice, your way.

Ten rooms. Home has a tile for each, with one live hint on it. Vocabulary is your word cards. Practise mixes everything together. Listen plays French and asks what you understood; Read shows French and asks the same. Grammar teaches a topic and then practises it; Verbs drills conjugation on its own. Walkman is a tape you just listen to. Sounds trains your ear. Story is a serial written for you, and Radio is a short episode each day. Underneath, Mistakes gathers what keeps catching you out, and Statistics opens with the points of the last two weeks, words and verbs together (tap a day to see how it split), then shows one part at a time — Words, Verbs or Other, and it remembers which. Words: how their scores spread from shaky to solid (tap a bar to see its words; choose a day, or press ▶, to see the chart as it was at the end of any day since you started), how many you know, your hardest words, each category, and your answers over the last two weeks. Verbs: the same score chart for your verb forms, your hardest forms (tap one to fill in its table), each tense and person with its average score and share right, and each kind of verb. Other: your Listen and Read levels and the rest. Every chart’s ⓘ says what it shows. Nothing there is a target.

Words from a song (in Vocabulary options): search for a French song, pick the right version (covers that sing the same words are grouped together), and Claude (Opus) reads it in the background, usually in under a minute — each word in its dictionary form: the infinitive for a verb, the masculine singular for an adjective, a noun with le, la or l’ and its gender. Names and sounds are left out. You untick anything you don’t want, and the rest become a deck named after the song — including the words you already have, which join it as they are: they keep their own category and your progress on them, so practising the song covers all of it. Each word joins your practice once it has been recorded, before your other new words or mixed in with them, as you choose in the options. The song’s page says how much of what is sung you already know, and Read the song shows it line by line: › at a line’s start gives its meaning, and a tapped word its note. The lyrics stay on your Mac.

Story is a serial in French, one episode at a time, written for you by Claude (Opus) from what you know: nearly every word is one you practise or have understood in Listen and Read, with a handful of new words and one grammar point a step ahead. Tap a word for its meaning (a new one says so), › for a line’s, listen to the episode read aloud — in French only, or each line in French and then in English, as you choose under the speaker; the line playing is marked, it keeps playing with your phone locked, and pausing or tapping a word stops it at that line — and answer three questions. When you finish, the next episode is written: a step harder if you looked up little and answered everything right, a step easier if you looked up a lot or missed questions, and the words you looked up come back in it. Writing takes a few minutes.

Radio is one episode a day, a few minutes long, made on your Mac: today’s words in sentences, each followed by its meaning; the latest story episode, line by line with its English; and one grammar point, explained in English with French examples. Like the Walkman it keeps playing with your phone locked, and the lock-screen buttons skip a part at a time. A new episode is ready each morning after 04:00.

Verbs drills conjugation on its own, with the verbs in your vocabulary (song verbs too): choose your verbs (the ones you have started, the 20 most common, or your own pick) and the tenses — présent, passé composé, imparfait, futur. Cards works like your vocabulary cards: a verb, a tense and a person; say the form to yourself, tap to turn the card (it shows the form and the whole tense), then tap left if you didn’t know it and right if you did (or Space, then the arrow keys). Your mark counts for that form just like a typed answer: it colours the verb map and decides which forms come up next, and a form you missed comes back a few cards later. Cards never run out: do as many as you like and leave whenever you want. Quick forms asks one form at a time and, like Cards, never ends — more forms are chosen as you go, from how your answers are going: a wrong answer says which mistake it was (another person’s form, another tense, a doubled letter, the wrong helper), shows the whole tense with the endings lit, and comes back once, a few forms later. Accents are not required, here or in Whole table: the right letters with an accent missing or wrong count as right, and the form is shown with its accents. Whole table asks all six persons of one verb at once. Next table (the default) picks the table for you from the verbs and tenses you chose, tables with shaky or untried forms most often and never one of the last five, and after Check moves on to the next; Choose lets you pick the verb and tense yourself, as does Fill in this table on a verb’s page. By ear plays a short phrase and asks which tense it is, or whether it is about one person or more — many endings are silent, so sometimes the honest answer is “can’t tell by ear”. Every form has a score, like a word: +2 for a typed answer right and −3 wrong (Quick forms, Whole table), +1 or −2 for your own mark (Cards, Walkman verbs), fading back towards 0 over a few weeks; By ear does not count. Cards, Quick forms, Next table and the Walkman verb tape all pick by it, the lowest scores most often, and Statistics shows the scores day by day, the points of each day, the average by tense and by person, and your five hardest forms. The verb map shows every form you have practised, one square per person, from shaky to solid; tap one to practise its table. Learn the tenses has a page for each tense: what it is for, the pattern on three model verbs with the changing part lit, your own verbs sorted by what is regular about them there (irregular verbs, stem changes, verbs like finir, partir or prendre, être verbs, irregular participles and futur stems), and the full rules from Grammar. Tap any verb, or look one up, to see it in all four tenses. Claude (Opus) writes each verb’s table once, and Echo checks it against the rules French is regular about before using it; the By ear phrases are recorded and checked on your Mac in the background.

Sounds trains your ear on pairs of phrases that differ by one sound — le chat and les chats, tu and tout, bon and banc. Echo plays one; you choose which you heard, then can hear both. The pairs you confuse come back first. Every recording was checked to be heard as itself and not its twin; a pair whose recording failed that check is left out.

When your Mac can’t be reached — a dropped connection, or the Mac too busy to answer — Echo says so and keeps trying by itself (after 2, 4, 8 and 15 seconds, then every 30), and at once when your phone is back online or you return to the app. What you were doing waits for it: an answer is kept and saved as soon as the Mac answers, and never counted twice. Retry now tries straight away.

Colours and hooks. Nouns are coloured by gender — blue for masculine, pink for feminine — wherever Echo shows French (switch it off in Vocabulary options). A word you keep missing gets a short memory hook on its card, written by Claude, to help it stick.

Walkman plays a French word, pauses so you can find its meaning, says the English, then the French again — word after word, and it keeps playing with your phone locked. Every eighth word is new, heard three times: again about 10 words later and once more about 20 after that, so it never comes straight back. The rest are words you have started, picked one of two ways, chosen under the player; a third choice plays sentences instead of words. By score (the default): every word has a score that rises when you recall it (+2) or hear it to the end on the Walkman (+0.5), drops when you miss it (−3), and fades by half every three weeks; words with low scores are far likelier to be picked — a word at −6 about 130 times likelier than one at +6 — but any word can come up. By groups: words you are learning first (the ones you miss most at the front), then those due for review, then known words about to fade. Either way a word waits at least 15 others before it returns, and listening never changes your vocabulary schedule. Sentences plays the Listen and Read sentences up to your Read level the same way — French, a pause to understand it, the English, the French again — each once per tape, with sentences holding your due and shakiest words likelier to come first; hearing them changes no scores. What you heard lists the last 50 finished words and sentences, newest first: the speaker plays one again (the tape pauses and carries on), ✓ and ✗ let you say afterwards whether you knew it — counted like a recall (+2) or a miss (−3) in its score, tap again to take it back — and a word you haven’t started can go straight into your Vocabulary practice. Marks move only the Walkman score, never your vocabulary schedule.

Walkman verbs (the fourth choice under the player) drills conjugation by ear: you hear a verb, a tense and a person — the person in English (“the they-form”), because il and ils sound the same — then a pause to say the form in your head, then the form twice. It uses the 20 most common verbs, in the tenses you choose under the player; they are the same tenses as in Verbs, so changing one changes both. Forms you got wrong come early, solid ones late. In What you heard, ✓ or ✗ on a form counts like a Verbs card, and once marked it stays marked.

Listen and Read never end. Each question is chosen for you from graded French — single sentences, short dialogues and short texts, A1 towards B1. You choose the meaning, type a word you heard, write down the whole sentence, or answer a question about a passage. The small A1 · A2 · B1 label shows the level Echo is choosing from: it drifts up while you keep answering well and eases back when you don’t. Nothing is scored or counted.

In Listen the recording plays by itself when the question appears, and a passage plays line by line with the line number while it runs. The speaker button pauses and continues; Line again replays just the line you last heard and stops after it, and From the start plays it all again. The French stays hidden until you answer. If your browser blocks playback, a large play button appears — tap it once and Echo carries on by itself.

Tap a word. Every French word Echo has a meaning for is underlined: tap it to see what it means in that sentence, and Add to my words puts it into your vocabulary practice (it tells you if the word is already there, or makes it due again). In Read, looking a word up before you answer counts as help, so a right answer is recorded as “with help”. After you answer, tap as freely as you like.

After each answer you see whether you were right, the expected answer — for a dictation, the words you missed — a short explanation, and the whole French with its English, line by line. One button continues; Enter does the same.

Practise is one endless stream that alternates understanding, vocabulary and grammar: a comprehension question, a vocabulary card, another comprehension question, a grammar question, skipping anything with nothing waiting. The small label says which you are in. Grammar here is a quiet round across the topics you have already touched, and it never shows a score.

Mistakes gathers what keeps catching you out: a grammar topic you miss, words you keep missing, sentences you didn’t catch. Tap a group to practise just that — a grammar group alternates its grammar questions with the sentences that use it.

Vocabulary. Recall the word, then tap to reveal its translation. Tap the left half if you didn’t know it, or the right half if you did. Space or Enter reveals; the arrow keys grade. Undo takes back your latest answer.

Two successful recalls after your latest miss finish a practice cycle; a miss resets the count to zero and moves the card to the failed stack. FSRS schedules the next review. Each direction has its own memory. Category, daily allowance and pronunciation speed live in Vocabulary options.

Your working deck has three stacks: unseen, last answered incorrectly, and last answered correctly. Unseen counts the first stack plus new words available within your daily allowance. Seen counts the other two stacks together. Finished cards count in neither. Undo restores the card, its stack and the counts.

The first ten unseen cards come first. After that, how often an unseen card comes depends on how many seen cards are waiting: up to three in four while the pile is small, one in two at 25 seen, one in three at 33, one in five at 41, and never fewer than one in ten — so the pile fills to about 25–35 and stays there, a little higher on a day of many misses, and new cards never stop. Among seen cards, failed ones come twice as often as successful ones; empty stacks are skipped. A card you just missed waits for at least three others; one you got right waits until every other seen card has had a turn (30 cards when 30 are seen; at least 5, at most 40), so seeing it again really tests you. If every seen card is still waiting, the one that has waited longest comes back rather than an extra new word, so new words keep to their share; the waits shorten further only near the end, when nothing else is left. Every card enters or re-enters a stack at a random position. The daily allowance renews at 04:00 on your Mac; unfinished cards keep their stacks and progress.

Endless (Practice: Daily or Endless, in Vocabulary options) goes over the words you have already started, as long as you like, with no unseen or seen stacks and never a new word. It picks them the way the Walkman does: the lower a word’s score, the likelier it comes — so the ones you find hard come most — and a word waits at least ten others before it comes back. Knowing a word adds 1 to its score and missing it takes 1 off, the same score the Walkman and Statistics use; your daily stacks, counts and review schedule stay exactly as they were. Undo takes back the last answer. It follows the direction and category you have chosen.

Extra practice. In Vocabulary options, or when you reach the end, add +15, +20, +30 or any number of new words for today only; your daily allowance is unchanged and the extra ends at 04:00. You can also review finished words early — 15, 20, 30 or any number of the ones due soonest, or the ones you missed most. Asking for more than are available takes all of them. An early review is a real review: finishing it updates the word’s schedule, with a smaller step because it came early.

Your words (the search button in Vocabulary) lists every word. Choose Studied to see the words you have practised, most recent first, with when you last studied each, how often you missed it and its next review. Hide translations lets you test yourself and reveal each answer; checking the list never changes your progress.

Word families (the family button in Vocabulary) groups words built from one another — la cuisine, cuisiner, le cuisinier — with a note on how each is built. It lists the families where you already know a word, fullest first; tap one to see its words, hear them, and add one you haven’t started. Because a word whose relative you know is half known, up to half of each day’s new words come from families you have started; the rest follow the usual order, so every category keeps moving. Word building (at the top of Grammar, or from Word families) shows the dozen patterns these families are made by — -er makes the verb, -eur the person who does it, in- the opposite, two words joined into one — each with pairs of your own words; tap a word to hear it, read how it is built, or add it.

Grammar & sentences. Seven units take you from A1 towards B1. Open a topic to read its rule, then practise a round of 10 questions: build, rewrite, complete, repair, choose, translate and conjugate. Check your answer, try again with specific feedback, or ask for a hint or the rule; each round records what you did on your own, with help, or need to revisit. Ctrl/⌘ + Enter checks or continues. Once a question is answered, a speaker button reads the correct French aloud where a recording exists.

Tests. A topic test asks 15 questions across the whole topic, and each unit ends with a test of 20 questions across its topics. Tests have no hints, rule or shown answers, and each question takes one try; you see the right answer and why straight after. Your best and latest scores are kept, and anything you miss in a test comes back first the next time you practise that topic.

Audio is your choice in Vocabulary. It starts off. Turn Audio on to hear the French recording each time you explicitly reveal a card, in either direction. Turn on Listen (French → English) to hear each word instead of reading it. Listen and Read always play their recordings; the pronunciation speed in Vocabulary options — normal or slower — applies everywhere.

Your vocabulary progress, grammar rounds, comprehension levels and mistakes live on your Mac, shared by your phone and desktop. Your direction, category and audio choices are remembered on this device. Echo always opens on Home, and Back returns to the previous screen.
